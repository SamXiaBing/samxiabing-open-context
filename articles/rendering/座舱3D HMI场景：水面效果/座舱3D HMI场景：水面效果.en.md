> In some car-model home screens, the HMI design places a water surface in the scene — a way to make the whole scene feel more spacious, richer in detail, and more dynamic. It isn't used that often, but I'd like to walk through how this kind of water is done.


![](./image.png)


# Understanding the Options

For water reflections under the URP pipeline, there are a few reference options:

- Boat Attack: an official Unity sample game.
- Stylized Water: a commercial plugin for stylized water rendering.
- Crest Ocean System: a plugin for generating highly realistic ocean scenes.

Looking at these sample projects and plugins, calm water almost always goes with planar reflection — Boat Attack and Stylized Water, for example. Planar reflection re-renders the entire scene once with a virtual camera, stores the result as a reflection map, and samples that map while shading the water surface.

Crest is built for rolling ocean waves. The water surface itself carries vertex animation, so planar reflection doesn't apply there; it uses real-time reflection probes instead, relying on the cubemaps generated by those probes to provide approximate reflections.

As for screen-space reflection (SSR), they either don't use it or don't use it as the primary approach. SSR doesn't render the scene again — it computes purely from the current frame's data, so it can only reflect what is visible on screen.

# Choosing the Approach

Back to our own environment constraints. The water surface in the Launcher has three premises:

**One, the water surface is a pure plane.** The geometry never moves; ripples come from normal-map perturbation at the pixel level. This means the assumption behind planar reflection holds.

**Two, a top-down view.** The camera looks at the water from above and at an angle most of the time, and a large share of the reflected view directions point toward the sky and off-screen. Screen-space reflection would struggle to cover that.

**Three, head-unit hardware performance.** The cost of rendering the reflection map has to be tunable.

So the choice matches the industry mainstream: planar reflection. The remaining work is **controlling the cost.**

# The Implementation


![](./image.png)


**1. Build the reflection matrix**

```csharp
// Plane as a Vector4: normal + distance from the plane to the origin
Vector4 plane = new Vector4(up.x, up.y, up.z,
    -Vector3.Dot(up, position) - heightOffset);
reflectionMatrix = CalculateReflectMatrix(plane);
```

The water surface works like an infinitely large mirror, and the reflection matrix flips the world across that mirror. The implementation is the standard planar-reflection derivation. At render time, the reflected region is limited by the camera frustum. The `heightOffset` parameter lets the reflection plane shift up or down relative to the water surface for fine-tuning.

**2. The camera in the mirrored world**

```csharp
Matrix4x4 reflWorldToCam = originWorldToCam * reflectionMatrix;
```

No Camera object or component is ever created. Left-multiplying the main camera's view matrix by the reflection matrix is equivalent to moving the camera into the mirrored world; rendering with this set of matrices draws exactly what the water surface should mirror.

**3. Oblique near clip: cut off what's underwater**

```csharp
Vector4 clipNormal = reflWorldToCam.MultiplyVector(planeUp);
Vector4 clipPlane = new Vector4(clipNormal.x, clipNormal.y, clipNormal.z,
    -Vector3.Dot(reflWorldToCam.MultiplyPoint(positionOnPlane), clipNormal));
var oblique = clipPlane * (2.0f / Vector4.Dot(clipPlane,
    refProjection.inverse * new Vector4(sgn(clipPlane.x), sgn(clipPlane.y), 1f, 1f)));
refProjection[2]  = oblique.x - refProjection[3];
refProjection[6]  = oblique.y - refProjection[7];
```

The mirrored camera shoots from below the water looking up. To keep underwater content out of the reflection, the projection matrix's near clip plane is bent until it aligns with the water surface — the near clip plane ends up sitting exactly on the water. This is known as the oblique near clip plane.

Unity's built-in Camera.CalculateObliqueMatrix(clipPlane) wraps exactly the operations above and hands you the result directly.

**4. Flip backface culling**

```csharp
cmd.SetViewProjectionMatrices(reflWorldToCam, refProjection);
cmd.SetInvertCulling(true);
cmd.DrawRendererList(context.CreateRendererList(ref rendererListParams));
```

This is where the command buffer work starts: set the view-projection matrices, turn on inverted culling, and draw a batch of objects. After the mirror transform, a triangle's vertex winding flips as well — a front face wound clockwise becomes counterclockwise in the mirror, gets treated as a back face, and gets culled, which would turn the entire mirrored world inside out. So we enable inverted culling and flip the culling rule along with it.

**5. Dual Kawase blur**

```csharp
// Two downsample stages then two upsample stages — four fullscreen blits in total, with the result written back to the original RT
cmd.SetGlobalFloat(BlurOffsetX, blurRadiusH * blurRadius);
cmd.SetGlobalFloat(BlurOffsetY, blurRadiusV * blurRadius);
DrawFullScreenTriangle(cmd, reflRT, blurTempRT[0], blurMat, 1);   // Full resolution → 1/2
DrawFullScreenTriangle(cmd, blurTempRT[0], blurTempRT[1], blurMat, 1);   // 1/2 → 1/4
DrawFullScreenTriangle(cmd, blurTempRT[1], blurTempRT[0], blurMat, 2);   // 1/4 → 1/2
DrawFullScreenTriangle(cmd, blurTempRT[0], reflRT, blurMat, 2);   // 1/2 → full resolution
```

The reflection map also needs a blur pass — Dual Kawase in our case.

**6. Bind the result to a global texture**

```csharp
cmd.SetGlobalTexture(ReflectionTexID, m_FRPCamera.planarReflectionRTHandle.nameID);
```

The finished RT is assigned to the global texture `_ReflectionTex` via `SetGlobalTexture`. The binding happens before the blur, and the last pass of Dual Kawase writes the blurred result back into that same RT, so the global reference never needs updating.

**7. Sample the water with screen coordinates**

```hlsl
// Screen coordinates are the sampling coordinates: the reflection camera shares the projection with the main camera
float2 reflUV = screenUV.xy / screenUV.w;

// Ripple perturbation: the normal's y component is added straight onto the sampling coordinates, so the reflection sways with the waves
reflUV += N.y;

float3 reflection = SAMPLE_TEXTURE2D(_ReflectionTex, sampler_ReflectionTex, reflUV).rgb;

// Fresnel: looking down at the water, the water color dominates; near grazing angles, the reflection takes over
float fresnel = pow(1.0 - saturate(dot(N, V)), 4.0);
float3 col = lerp(waterColor, reflection, fresnel);
```

A water pixel's sampling coordinate in the reflection map is simply its own screen coordinate — no conversion needed, because the reflection camera shares the same projection as the main camera; the only difference is that the view matrix is mirrored.

On top of that, a tiny offset is added to the sampling coordinate so the reflection sways along with the ripples. The offset comes from the water surface's normal — how exactly that normal is computed and how the ripples move is covered in the next section.

Finally, Fresnel: the reflection is weak when you look straight down at the water, and grows stronger as the view grazes the surface. A lerp blends out the final pixel color.

# Water Ripple Disturbance

The ripples on the water involve no vertex animation — the mesh stays perfectly flat, and all the motion happens at the normal-map level:

```hlsl
// The UV source is world-space XZ (independent of mesh UVs, which is why the tiling values are small decimals)
// Two Tiling And Offset nodes: one positive tiling, one negative, so the same scroll speed drives opposite flow directions
float2 uv1 = worldPos.xz * _NormalTiling1 + _Time.y * _OffsetSpeed;
float2 uv2 = worldPos.xz * _NormalTiling2 + _Time.y * _OffsetSpeed;
float3 N = NormalStrength(
    BlendNormal(UnpackNormal(SAMPLE_TEXTURE2D(_Normal_Map, uv1)),
                UnpackNormal(SAMPLE_TEXTURE2D(_Blend_Map, uv2))),
    _NormalMapStrength);
```

Concretely, two normal maps are stacked: _Normal_Map and _Blend_Map, tiled at 0.05 and -0.05 respectively, both scrolling over time with the same _OffsetSpeed. Using two maps is what keeps the ripples from looking repetitive. The resulting N has its y component added directly to the reflection sampling coordinates.

# Closing Thoughts

Earlier I said the cost has to be controlled, mainly through: scaling the reflection map's resolution (0.1x to 1.0x), and culling which objects participate in the reflection by Layer. Alternatively, you can render only a few designated Layers (say, the skybox plus the distant mountains) and piece together an approximate reflection. The most aggressive option: if there's no risk of an interaction exposing the trick, pre-bake a static reflection image into a texture and skip the extra render pass entirely.
